## What it is

The Notre Dame Global Adaptation Initiative (ND-GAIN) scores countries on two dimensions: how vulnerable they are to climate change, and how ready they are to adapt. Readiness is the second. It measures a country's ability to use investment and turn it into adaptation on the ground ([ND-GAIN methodology](https://gain.nd.edu/our-work/country-index/methodology/)). It combines nine indicators in three groups:

- **economic:** whether the business climate can absorb investment
- **governance:** institutions and stability
- **social:** inequality, education, internet and phone networks, innovation

Scores run from 0 to 1, higher is better.

There are limits. Missing data are filled by interpolation ([ND-GAIN data](https://gain.nd.edu/our-work/country-index/download-data/)), so recent years for poorly measured countries are partly guesses. The index measures capacity to adapt, not whether adaptation actually happens. Some sharp changes may come from revisions to the underlying indicators rather than real change on the ground.

## Why it matters

Climate shocks, pandemics and price spikes hit every country. What separates a hard year from a collapse is whether the country can mobilise money, build defences, run relief and keep services going. That ability is what readiness tries to capture. It overlaps with government effectiveness but adds economic and social capacity.

## Where it stands

In the 2026 release, France scores 0.639 for 2024, down from 0.661 in 2015. Singapore is highest at 0.749, and the Central African Republic lowest at 0.137. The median country scores 0.39 ([ND-GAIN data](https://gain.nd.edu/our-work/country-index/download-data/)). Several rich countries fell sharply from 2015 to 2024, Norway from 0.800 to 0.700 and the United Kingdom from 0.714 to 0.653, so read trends with care.

Weighted by population, the world averages 0.402. No one projects readiness. If every country kept its 2024 score and populations changed as the UN projects ([WPP 2024](https://population.un.org/wpp/assets/Excel%20Files/1_Indicator%20(Standard)/CSV_FILES/WPP2024_Demographic_Indicators_Medium.csv.gz)), the average would fall to 0.359 by 2100. The share of people living in countries below 0.25 would rise from 8.4% to 16.2%. This is our own calculation, not a forecast. It shows that population growth is fastest where readiness is lowest.

## The lines that matter

Both lines are our judgment, set from today's spread of scores.

- **0.40 (world median).** Below it, a country is less able than the typical one to protect its people from shocks. We put France's chance of falling below 0.40 by 2100 at about 6%. For the population-weighted world average, it is about 50%: population shifts push it down, while progress in middle-income countries pushes it up.
- **0.25 (bottom tenth).** Many countries here are in conflict or very poor, such as Chad, Somalia, Haiti and DR Congo. Money for adaptation rarely becomes protection. We put France's chance at about 2% and the world average's at about 3%.

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

Superintelligence could raise readiness in the poorest countries. It can improve forecasting, early warning, planning and the delivery of aid, and much of that can run on mobile phones. It could also help countries design cheaper defences such as better seeds, cooling and flood warning. It cannot replace stable institutions or make investors trust a country at war. The social and governance parts of readiness change on the timescale of politics, not software.
