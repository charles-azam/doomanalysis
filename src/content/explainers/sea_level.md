## What it is

The average height of the world's ocean surface, measured by satellites since January 1993. It rises for two reasons: seawater expands as it warms, and melting glaciers and ice sheets add water.

## Why it matters

Sea-level rise works through floods more than through slow drowning. A higher baseline means storms and high tides reach further inland. People living less than 1 m above today's high-tide line number about 230 million ([Kulp and Strauss 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-12808-z)). The same study found that by 2100, under high emissions, up to 630 million people live on land below projected yearly flood levels.

Sea level also has a long memory. It keeps rising for centuries after warming stops because the deep ocean keeps warming and ice sheets keep losing mass. Peak warming of 2 °C commits about 2–6 m of rise over 2,000 years; 3 °C commits 4–10 m ([IPCC AR6, Chapter 9](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-9/)).

## Where it stands

At the end of 2025, sea level was about **11 cm** higher than in January 1993. The pace has nearly doubled: 2.65 mm a year over 1993–2011, then 4.75 mm a year over 2012–2025 ([WMO](https://library.wmo.int/records/item/69807-state-of-the-global-climate-2025)).

For 2100, relative to the 1995–2014 average, the IPCC projects (likely ranges, [via the Hong Kong Observatory](https://www.hko.gov.hk/en/climate_change/proj_global_sea_level.htm)):
- intermediate emissions (SSP2-4.5, close to today's policies): **56 cm (44–76)**
- low emissions (SSP1-2.6): 44 cm (32–62)
- very high emissions (SSP5-8.5): 77 cm (63–101)

These ranges leave out poorly understood ice-sheet collapse processes. Under high emissions those could add more than a metre, and a rise approaching 2 m by 2100 cannot be ruled out (IPCC AR6). In a survey of ice-sheet experts, the ice-sheet share alone at 2 °C had a median of 26 cm and a 95th percentile of 81 cm ([Bamber et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1817205116)).

## The lines that matter

No agency defines official thresholds, so we use two round numbers relative to 1995–2014. Add about 3 cm to compare with the 1993 starting point.

- **50 cm:** by 2100, floods that used to come once a century would occur every year at roughly 60% (low emissions) to 82% (very high emissions) of the world's tide gauges (IPCC AR6, Chapter 9). Chance of passing 50 cm by 2100: about 68%, derived from the IPCC intermediate projection.
- **1 m:** reaches land where about 230 million people now live. A fit to the IPCC's medium-confidence projection gives about 2% under intermediate emissions; we raise it to about 5% for ice-sheet surprises (judgment).

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

SI can make adaptation cheaper and smarter: better flood forecasts, cheaper sea walls and pumps, planned relocation, and building codes tuned to local risk. Faster emission cuts would also slow the rise later in the century.

SI cannot stop the rise already committed. The ocean has absorbed heat that will keep expanding it, and ice sheets respond over centuries. Even with zero emissions tomorrow, seas keep rising past 2100. The useful question is how fast, and whether coasts adapt in time.
