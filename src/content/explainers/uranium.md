## Why it matters here

Nuclear power gives 8.8% of the world's electricity ([Energy Institute](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmgsites/au/pdf/2026/statistical-review-of-world-energy.pdf)). Its fuel is compact: reactors need about 64,500 tonnes of uranium a year ([NEA](https://oecd-nea.org/jcms/pl_121582/adequate-uranium-resources-available-but-sustained-investment-essential-to-support-global-nuclear-capacity-growth)) for 31 EJ of supply, while coal takes 9.05 billion tonnes for 181 EJ ([Energy Institute](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmgsites/au/pdf/2026/statistical-review-of-world-energy.pdf)). That is 64,500 ÷ 31 ≈ 2,100 tonnes per EJ against 9.05 billion ÷ 181 = 50 million, about 24,000 times less mass, which makes stockpiles easy. Our judgment: shortage is a distant limit, and a cut-off is felt slowly.

## In numbers

- **Resources.** 8.1 million tonnes recoverable below $260/kg ÷ 64,500 t a year = 126 years ([NEA](https://oecd-nea.org/jcms/pl_121582/adequate-uranium-resources-available-but-sustained-investment-essential-to-support-global-nuclear-capacity-growth)). Below $130/kg: 5.86 million ÷ 64,500 = 91 years ([WNA](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/nuclear-fuel-cycle/uranium-resources/supply-of-uranium)).
- **Growth.** If needs reach 143,900 t a year in 2050, the Red Book's high case, the same resources last 8.1 million ÷ 143,900 = 56 years ([NEA](https://oecd-nea.org/jcms/pl_121582/adequate-uranium-resources-available-but-sustained-investment-essential-to-support-global-nuclear-capacity-growth)).
- **Fast reactors** use uranium about 60 times more efficiently ([WNA](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/current-and-future-generation/fast-neutron-reactors)): 8.1 million × 60 ÷ 64,500 ≈ 7,500 years. Only Russia runs commercial-scale ones.
- **Seawater** holds over 4 billion tons ([PNNL](https://www.pnnl.gov/news-media/advances-extracting-uranium-seawater-announced-special-issue)): 4 billion ÷ 64,500 ≈ 62,000 years. Past cost estimates, $300 to $610/kg ([WNA](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/nuclear-fuel-cycle/uranium-resources/supply-of-uranium)), sit above the Red Book's top category.
- **Importers.** Kazakhstan mined 39% of the world's uranium in 2024, 23,270 of 60,213 tonnes ([WNA](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/nuclear-fuel-cycle/mining-of-uranium/world-uranium-mining-production)). Rosatom holds 43% of enrichment capacity (29,133 of 68,076 thousand separative work units, the measure of enrichment effort) ([WNA](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/nuclear-fuel-cycle/conversion-enrichment-and-fabrication/uranium-enrichment)). Russia supplied 16% of EU uranium deliveries in 2025 ([World Nuclear News](https://world-nuclear-news.org/articles/us-eu-bodies-report-on-nuclear-fuel-market-situation)).

## The lines on the card

- **56 years:** today's resources at the Red Book's high demand for 2050.
- **7,500 years:** today's resources used in fast reactors.
- **62,000 years:** seawater uranium at today's use.

## Where the 2100 odds come from

No source publishes odds for 2100. The Red Book finds resources sufficient for even its highest demand through 2050 ([NEA](https://oecd-nea.org/jcms/pl_121582/adequate-uranium-resources-available-but-sustained-investment-essential-to-support-global-nuclear-capacity-growth)). The value in 2100 depends on how many reactors are built, whether fast reactors spread and what exploration finds.

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

It could speed fast-reactor design and cheaper seawater extraction. Licensing and building reactors and enrichment plants still takes a decade or more.
