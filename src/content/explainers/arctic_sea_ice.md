## What it is

Each September the Arctic Ocean's floating ice shrinks to its smallest area of the year. Satellites have measured that minimum since 1979. The site shows the September minimum extent, in millions of square kilometres.

## Why it matters

Sea ice is not a direct threat to food or water for most people. It matters as a gauge and an amplifier. Its loss tracks warming closely: the IPCC finds Arctic summer ice area falls almost in a straight line with global temperature and cumulative CO₂ emissions ([IPCC AR6, Chapter 9](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-9/)). Open water absorbs more sunlight than ice, which adds to Arctic warming. The [Global Tipping Points Report 2025](https://wwfes.awsassets.panda.org/downloads/gtp_summary_report_2025-v4-pages-lo-res--1-.pdf) also flags a possible vicious cycle in which ice loss and permafrost thaw feed each other.

## Where it stands

The September 2026 minimum was **4.6 million km²**, tied with 2025, 2008 and 2010 for the 10th-lowest on record ([NASA/NSIDC](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/arctic-sea-ice-shrinks-to-its-2026-minimum/)). Averaged over the whole year, 2025 had the lowest or second-lowest Arctic ice extent in the satellite era, and the lowest winter maximum on record ([WMO](https://library.wmo.int/records/item/69807-state-of-the-global-climate-2025)). Year-to-year swings are large, so single years say little; the long-term decline is clear.

Looking ahead, the IPCC says the Arctic is likely to be practically ice-free in September at least once before 2050, under every scenario it assessed ([IPCC AR6](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf)). A 2024 review puts the first ice-free September possibly in the 2020s or 2030s and likely by mid-century, with climate models showing consistently ice-free Septembers starting between 2035 and 2067 ([Jahn, Holland and Kay 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43017-023-00515-9)). At the roughly 2–3 °C that today's policies imply, the Arctic would be practically ice-free throughout September in most years (IPCC AR6, Chapter 9).

## The lines that matter

- **1 million km², "ice-free":** scientists use this, not zero; it is less than 20% of a typical 1980s September minimum ([CU Boulder](https://colorado.edu/today/2024/03/05/arctic-could-become-ice-free-within-decade)). One model study found that holding warming to 1.5 °C cuts the chance of any ice-free summer by 2100 from 100% to 30% compared with 2 °C ([Jahn 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-018-0127-8)); the author calls this a lower bound. Combining those odds with our warming forecast ([CAT](https://climateactiontracker.org/documents/1451/CAT_2026-11-12_GlobalUpdate_COP31.pdf)) gives about a **97%** chance of at least one ice-free September before 2100 (derived).

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

Sea ice follows temperature, so SI helps only to the extent it lowers warming. There is one piece of good news: the IPCC finds no tipping point for summer sea-ice loss. If the planet cooled again, the ice would grow back. But Jahn (2018) finds it would only recover to today's level if CO₂ fell below present-day concentrations, which means large-scale removal over many decades.

SI cannot refreeze the Arctic in the next few decades. The ice-free Septembers expected before 2050 are driven by warming that has largely already happened.
