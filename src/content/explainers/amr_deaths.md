## What it is

Deaths caused by bacteria that no longer respond to the antibiotics meant to kill them. The main estimate comes from the GRAM study (Global Research on Antimicrobial Resistance). It gives two numbers. **Attributable** deaths would not have happened if the infection had been drug-sensitive. **Associated** deaths are all deaths in which a resistant infection played a part. This indicator uses attributable deaths, the stricter count.

## Why it matters

Modern medicine rests on antibiotics. Caesarean sections, hip replacements, chemotherapy and intensive care are only safe because infections can be treated. As resistance spreads, common infections become harder or impossible to cure. That raises the cost of health care and lowers what it can do. Resistance rarely kills fast or everywhere at once. Its damage is a slow drag on the health essential, not a sudden collapse.

## Where it stands

In 2021, 1.14 million deaths (95% range 1.00–1.28 million) were attributable to bacterial AMR, and 4.71 million were associated with it ([GRAM, Lancet 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(24)01867-1)). Attributable deaths were 1.06 million in 1990 ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1057804)). The trend splits by age. Since 1990, AMR deaths fell by more than half among children under five, which GRAM credits to infection prevention. They rose by over 80% among adults aged 70 and over.

GRAM's reference forecast for 2050 is 1.91 million attributable deaths a year (range 1.56–2.26 million) and 8.22 million associated deaths. Over 2025–2050 that adds up to more than 39 million attributable deaths. The rise is concentrated in older people: those aged 70 and over make up about 66% of the attributable deaths forecast for 2050. Two alternative scenarios show what action could do. Better treatment of severe infections and better access to antibiotics could avert 92 million deaths over 2025–2050. A new pipeline of drugs against Gram-negative bacteria, a group that is especially hard to treat, could avert 11.1 million AMR deaths. No published forecast goes beyond 2050.

## The lines that matter

- **2 million attributable deaths a year (judgment).** This is just above GRAM's 2050 central forecast and near the top of its range. Crossing it before 2050 would mean resistance is outrunning the reference path.
- **10 million deaths a year.** The 2016 O'Neill review warned that AMR could kill 10 million people a year by 2050 if nothing was done ([O'Neill 2016](https://amr-review.org/sites/default/files/160525_Final%20paper_with%20cover.pdf)). That was a warning about a world that takes no action. GRAM's later, more detailed work, built on 520 million records, puts even associated deaths at 8.22 million in 2050, and attributable deaths far lower.

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

AMR is one of the most promising areas for SI. AI tools already help screen molecules for antibacterial activity. SI could speed discovery, rapid diagnostics and the targeting of antibiotic use. Three limits remain. Each new drug still needs years of human trials. Bacteria evolve resistance to every new drug, so stewardship never ends. Many deaths are in places without basic diagnosis, clean water or access to existing antibiotics, which is a problem of money and health systems more than of science.
