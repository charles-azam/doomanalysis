## What it is

This indicator is the share of people living in a household with an electricity connection ([World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/EG.ELC.ACCS.ZS)). Most of the data come from household surveys and censuses, with models filling the gaps between survey years. It is a yes-or-no measure. The official [SDG metadata](https://unstats.un.org/sdgs/metadata/files/Metadata-07-01-01.pdf) warns that a connection does not guarantee power that is reliable, of decent quality, or affordable. A home with two hours of power a day and frequent blackouts counts the same as one in Paris. The World Bank's Multi-Tier Framework measures hours, reliability and cost, but it is not yet used everywhere.

## Why it matters

Electricity is the energy essential and a precondition for the others. It pumps and treats water, keeps vaccines and food cold, lights clinics, and powers phones and payments. Abundance requires near-universal, reliable supply. Losing it at scale is one of the fastest routes to the other essentials failing too.

## Where it stands

The world reached 91.9% in 2024, up from 83.5% in 2010 ([World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/EG.ELC.ACCS.ZS)). That still leaves 655 million people without electricity. About 89% of them live in Africa, and 80% live in just 22 countries, 19 of them African ([Tracking SDG7 2026](https://www.seforall.org/system/files/2026-07/SEforALL%20Tracking%20SDG7%20-%20The%20Energy%20Progress%20Report%202026_Executive%20Version_22Jul2026-2.pdf)). Africa's access rate rose from 45% to 62% since 2010, but population growth kept the number without power almost flat.

France is at 100%, Nigeria at 62.5%, Ethiopia at 56.6% and DR Congo at 22.5% ([World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/EG.ELC.ACCS.ZS), 2024).

On current trends, about 644 million people will still lack power in 2030, about 92% of them in Africa. Universal access would mean connecting about 177 million people a year, at about USD 30 billion a year ([Tracking SDG7 2026](https://www.seforall.org/system/files/2026-07/SEforALL%20Tracking%20SDG7%20-%20The%20Energy%20Progress%20Report%202026_Executive%20Version_22Jul2026-2.pdf)). We found no published projection to 2100.

## The lines that matter

Both lines come from this site's own definitions, not from an agency.

- **90%.** Below it, abundance is impossible, because abundance requires 90% of people to have every essential. The world is just above this line. Our estimate is a 10% chance that it falls below 90% at some point before 2100, through stalled progress in fast-growing countries or a major global shock.
- **50%.** Below it, a majority lacks power: the collapse line, if it lasts five years. Our estimate for the world is 2%. DR Congo is already below it, but it never had majority access, so this is "never reached" rather than "collapsed".

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

Superintelligence could make solar panels, batteries and mini-grids cheaper, and it could plan and run grids better. Those are real gains. But the gap today is mostly rural and concentrated in a few low-income countries. In sub-Saharan Africa, 83% of town dwellers have power but only 33% of rural people do. The main shortfall is investment, about USD 30 billion a year, not missing technology. Superintelligence could help find the money and manage the projects. It cannot by itself lay cables in a war zone or make a bankrupt utility pay for upkeep.
