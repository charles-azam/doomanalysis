## What it is

The share of a country's economy spent on the five essentials, by households and governments together. We add up:

- **Food:** what households spend on food and soft drinks.
- **Energy:** home electricity, gas and heating fuel, plus fuel for cars.
- **Water:** water supply and sewerage bills.
- **Health:** all health spending, public and private.
- **Security:** the military plus police, courts, prisons and fire services.

We divide the sum by GDP, one year of the country's total output. No agency publishes this total. We built it from national accounts, the [World Bank's 2021 price survey](https://databank.worldbank.org/source/icp-2021), [WHO](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SH.XPD.CHEX.GD.ZS) and [SIPRI](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/MS.MIL.XPND.GD.ZS) data, plus a few judged pieces. The range adds business energy use and restaurant meals.

## Why it matters

It tests a bold claim. Elon Musk has predicted "universal high income" and said "There'll be no shortage of goods or services" ([Fox Business](https://foxbusiness.com/economy/musk-predicts-ai-create-universal-high-income-make-saving-money-unnecessary)). Tesla's 2025 plan calls the goal "sustainable abundance" ([Drive Tesla Canada](https://driveteslacanada.ca/news/tesla-master-plan-part-iv-ushers-in-age-of-ai-powered-sustainable-abundance/)). If essentials become nearly free, their cost should shrink to a small slice of income. This site's bar for abundance is under 10% of national income, averaged over 2096–2100.

## Where it stands

Our best estimates (with ranges) are:

- France 27% (26–34)
- United States 29% (28–34)
- Germany 27%, Sweden 25%, Japan 25%, United Kingdom 24%
- Norway 20%, lowered by large oil and gas revenues
- China 18.5%, India 31%, Brazil 31%, Nigeria 38%
- World 26% (24–36)

Poor countries spend mostly on food: 28% of GDP in Nigeria ([ICP 2021](https://databank.worldbank.org/source/icp-2021)). Rich countries spend mostly on health: 16.7% of GDP in the US in 2023 ([WHO](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SH.XPD.CHEX.GD.ZS)).

The parts move in opposite directions:

- **Food falls as incomes rise.** Americans spent 9.7% of disposable income on food in 2025, against 10.4% in 1997 ([USDA](https://ers.usda.gov/data-products/ag-and-food-statistics-charting-the-essentials/food-prices-and-spending)).
- **Health rises.** US national health spending is projected to grow from 18.4% of GDP in 2025 to 20.6% in 2034 ([CMS via CRFB](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/national-health-expenditures-continue-rise)).
- **Security rises.** NATO allies pledged 5% of GDP by 2035 ([NATO](https://www.nato.int/en/about-us/official-texts-and-resources/official-texts/2025/06/25/the-hague-summit-declaration)).
- **Energy is much bigger once businesses are counted.** Their energy use pushes the bill to 5.7% of US GDP ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=65965)) and nearly 10% worldwide ([IEA via SolarQuarter](https://solarquarter.com/2026/02/18/iea-2025-report-batteries-account-for-40-of-energy-patents-as-global-energy-spending-nears-10-of-gdp-report/)).

Nobody projects the total to 2100.

## The lines that matter

- **10%, the abundance line.** This is the site's own definition. Getting from 26% to 10% means essentials must become about 2.6 times cheaper relative to income. At today's [world income](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/NY.GDP.PCAP.PP.KD) of about $21,900 per person, 10% is about $2,200 a year for everything: food, power, water, care and protection.
- **50%, danger.** This is our judgment, not a published line. A country spending half its output just to stay fed, safe and healthy has little left to invest or absorb shocks. Nigeria is closest today.

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

It can attack the biggest costs, which are mostly labor: diagnosis, drug discovery and care in health, and farm work. Cheaper solar and batteries can shrink the energy bill. If superintelligence (SI) multiplies output while physical needs stay the same, the share falls on its own.

It cannot repeal the pattern of the past century: as people get richer, they buy more and better care, so health spending tends to grow. An arms race could push security spending up. Land and fresh water are physical limits. And "universal high income" is a political choice about who gets the output, not a technology.
