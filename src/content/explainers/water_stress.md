## What it is

Baseline water stress compares how much water a country withdraws each year for farms, industry and homes with how much renewable water its rivers and aquifers supply. The World Resources Institute's Aqueduct tool turns that ratio into a score from 0 to 5 ([Aqueduct 4.0](https://www.wri.org/data/aqueduct-40-country-rankings)):

- below 10% withdrawn: low
- 10–20%: low to medium
- 20–40%: medium to high
- 40–80%: high
- above 80%: **extremely high** (a score of 4 or more)

Country scores average the river basins in a country, weighted by water demand.

## Why it matters

Water stress is about margin. A country that uses 20% of its supply can ride out a dry year. A country that uses over 80% has almost no buffer: a short drought means rationing, crop losses, empty reservoirs and power cuts at plants that need cooling water. Water is one of the site's five essentials, and stress also spills into food and energy. Shared rivers add a security risk, because one country's dam is another's shortage.

## Where it stands

Twenty-five countries, home to a quarter of the world's population, face extremely high water stress every year. About 4 billion people, half the world, face high stress for at least one month a year ([WRI](https://www.wri.org/insights/highest-water-stressed-countries)).

| Country | Today | 2050, business as usual |
|---|---|---|
| Egypt | 4.85 | 4.46 |
| India | 4.11 | 4.02 |
| China | 2.80 | 2.76 |

| United States | 2.60 | 2.78 |
| France | 1.92 | 2.27 |
| Brazil | 1.04 | 1.24 |
| Nigeria | 0.80 | 1.51 |

By 2050, WRI projects global water demand up 20–25% and about 1 billion more people living with extremely high stress. The whole population of the Middle East and North Africa would face it, up from 83% today, and 31% of global GDP would be exposed to high stress, up from 24% in 2010. Demand in sub-Saharan Africa rises 163%, the fastest of any region (WRI). In the country data, the count rated extremely high rises from 25 to 29 under business as usual; Spain is one that crosses the line (our count from the Aqueduct data). WRI provides no projections beyond 2080.

Treat these as rough. WRI notes the indicators cannot be measured directly, are not validated, and ignore water piped between basins ([Kuzma et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.46830/writn.23.00061)). Globally, the Planetary Health Check finds the freshwater boundary already crossed ([PHC 2026](https://publications.pik-potsdam.de/pubman/item/item_35009/component/file_35022/PlanetaryHealthCheck2026.pdf)).

## The lines that matter

- **4, extremely high:** more than 80% of renewable supply withdrawn in a typical year, as defined by WRI. Aqueduct publishes scenario values, not probabilities, so there are no published odds of a country crossing it.

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

Much of water stress is an energy and engineering problem. Desalination, wastewater recycling, leak repair and precision irrigation all get cheaper with cheaper energy and better design. That is where SI could help most.

SI cannot make more rain fall in a basin or refill an emptied aquifer quickly. Desalinated water is still costly to pump inland and uphill. And rivers shared between rival states, such as the Nile or the Indus, are political problems first.
