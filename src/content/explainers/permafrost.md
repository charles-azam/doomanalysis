## What it is

Permafrost is ground that stays frozen all year, year after year. It underlies much of Siberia, Alaska and northern Canada, and it holds frozen plant and animal remains built up over millennia. The site's card shows the share of long-running Arctic boreholes in Alaska and Canada whose ground temperature set a record that year.

## Why it matters

When permafrost thaws, microbes break down that old organic matter and release CO₂ and methane. That adds warming on top of human emissions. The IPCC finds the loss of permafrost carbon is irreversible on centuries-long timescales ([IPCC AR6](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf)). Thaw also makes the ground sag and slump, damaging roads, pipelines and buildings across the Arctic.

There are two ways it can go. Gradual thaw follows warming. Abrupt thaw, where ice-rich ground collapses and forms lakes and slumps, adds about half again to the carbon released by gradual thaw ([Armstrong McKay et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abn7950)).

## Where it stands

In 2025, 15 of 24 monitored sites (62.5%) recorded their warmest ground temperatures on record ([BAMS State of the Climate in 2025](https://doi.org/10.1175/BAMS-D-26-0123.1)). Globally, permafrost warmed by 0.29 °C (0.17–0.41) between 2007 and 2016, and the thawed surface layer is thickening across the Arctic ([IPCC AR6, Chapter 9](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-9/)).

The IPCC's rule of thumb: the frozen volume in the top 3 m shrinks by about **25% for every 1 °C** of global warming, up to 4 °C. Relative to 1995–2014, that means losses of up to 50% at 1.5–2 °C, up to 75% at 2–3 °C, and up to 90% at 3–5 °C (IPCC AR6, Chapter 9). Applied to the 2.7 °C that current policies imply ([Climate Action Tracker](https://climateactiontracker.org/documents/1451/CAT_2026-11-12_GlobalUpdate_COP31.pdf)), the rule gives roughly **46%** of near-surface permafrost gone by the end of the century (our derivation; frozen ground lags air temperature, so the 2100 figure may be lower).

The record-share indicator on the card has no published projection. Under continued warming, most sites would keep setting records.

## The lines that matter

- **About 1.5 °C of global warming, abrupt thaw:** central estimate for widespread abrupt thaw of boreal permafrost (range 1–2.3 °C), unfolding over about 200 years (Armstrong McKay et al.). The [Global Tipping Points Report 2025](https://wwfes.awsassets.panda.org/downloads/gtp_summary_report_2025-v4-pages-lo-res--1-.pdf) judges that land permafrost likely has a tipping point around 1.5 °C. End-of-century warming above 1.5 °C is about 99% likely.
- **Half the near-surface permafrost lost (our line):** needs about 2.85 °C by the 25%-per-degree rule. Chance by 2100: about 35% (judgment).
- **Three-quarters lost (our line):** the IPCC's upper bound for 2–3 °C. Chance by 2100: about 5% (judgment).
- **About 4 °C, collapse:** central estimate for wholesale collapse of boreal permafrost, which could release 125–250 billion tonnes of carbon (Armstrong McKay et al.).

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

SI can lower future warming and help engineers design foundations, pipelines and towns for thawing ground. Better monitoring could spot abrupt thaw early.

It cannot refreeze permafrost at scale or recapture the carbon once microbes release it. The IPCC treats that loss as irreversible for centuries. Only limiting warming limits thaw.
