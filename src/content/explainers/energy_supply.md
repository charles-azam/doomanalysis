## Why it matters here

Energy runs every essential: pumps, tractors, fertiliser, hospitals, transport. Jean-Marc Jancovici argues that economic output roughly follows the energy a society can use, because machines multiply human effort: a truck engine has about 4,000 times its driver's power ([Jancovici](https://jancovici.com/en/energy-transition/societal-choices/a-couple-of-thoughts-on-the-energy-transition/)). Two Shift Project economists found, across 33 countries, that energy use drives GDP growth far more than standard models assume ([Giraud and Kahraman](https://ideas.repec.org/p/hal/cesptp/halshs-01151590.html)). On this view, less energy per person means lower living standards.

## In numbers

- **Today:** 72.9 GJ (gigajoules) per person in 2025: Africa 14.1, India 26.7, the EU 116, the US 270 ([Energy Institute](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmgsites/au/pdf/2026/statistical-review-of-world-energy.pdf)). That is 72.9 × 10⁹ J ÷ 31.5 million s = 2.3 kW per person, nonstop.
- **Not every fall is a loss.** Thermal power plants average 41% efficiency and only a third of supply ends as useful energy, so electric machines need less supply. The UK's supply per person fell 26% from 2015 to 2025, from 122 to 90 GJ ([Energy Institute](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmgsites/au/pdf/2026/statistical-review-of-world-energy.pdf)).
- **Holding today's level** for 10.18 billion people in 2100 ([UN](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/population-with-un-projections)) takes 72.9 × 10.18 = 742 EJ, 24% more than in 2025.

## What each level looks like

- **10–25% of today, level 4.** Even shared equally with the best technology, decent living needs 15.3 GJ of final energy per person ([Millward-Hopkins et al.](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/16764/1/1-s2.0-S0959378020307512-main.pdf)): 15.3 ÷ 72.9 = 21% of today's supply. Conversion losses raise that floor, so below 25% most people fall short.
- **25–50%, level 3.** Our judgment: the world averages India's level. Decent living needs about 9 to 36 GJ of final energy per person by region ([Kikstra et al.](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/17402/1/Kikstra_2021_Environ._Res._Lett._16_095006.pdf)), so with today's inequality over a billion fall short.
- **50–75%, level 2.** Our judgment: electrification absorbs much of the cut, but the poorest are priced out.
- **75–100%, level 1.** Our judgment: a fall the size of the UK's, absorbed by efficiency.
- **Above today, level 0.**

## Where the 2100 odds come from

No source publishes odds for 2100. The baseline scenarios the IPCC draws on range from demand peaking around 2060 (SSP1) to more than tripling (SSP5) ([Riahi et al.](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/13280/1/The%20shared%20socioeconomic%20pathways%20and%20their%20energy,%20land%20use,%20and%20greenhouse%20gas%20emissions%20implications.pdf)). In 2012 Jancovici expected oil output to peak around 2020 ([Jancovici](https://jancovici.com/en/energy-transition/societal-choices/a-couple-of-thoughts-on-the-energy-transition/)); in 2025 it was 10% above 2015 ([Energy Institute](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmgsites/au/pdf/2026/statistical-review-of-world-energy.pdf)).

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

Cheaper solar, storage and reactors could lift energy per person far above today. Plants, mines and grids still take decades to build.
