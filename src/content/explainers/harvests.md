## Why it matters here

Hot days cut the yields of most staple crops; smaller harvests raise food prices and push the poorest into hunger. This card, a channel of the warming card, measures the fall in world staple-crop yields against a world that does not warm.

## In numbers

- **Per degree.** A study of six staples across 12,658 regions, two-thirds of the world's crop calories, finds each 1 °C costs [about 120 kcal per person per day, 4.4% of today's recommended intake](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09085-w), after farmers adapt: 4 × 4.4 = 17.6% at 4 °C.
- **2100.** World yields fall [11% with moderate emissions and 24% with high emissions](https://impactlab.org/news-insights/climate-change-cuts-global-crop-yields-even-when-farmers-adapt/). With moderate emissions [maize loses 12.0%, wheat 13.5%, soybean 22.4% and rice 1.1%](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09085-w); with high emissions, adaptation and rising incomes remove 34% of the losses.
- **Today.** Climate trends to 2008 had cut calories from the top ten crops by [about 1%](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0217148). Against a pre-industrial climate, [maize lost 4.1%, wheat 1.8% and soybean 4.5%](https://www.naro.go.jp/english/laboratory/niaes/press/crop_production_loss/index.html) over 1981–2010. Droughts and heat waves were linked to [124 million more people in moderate or severe food insecurity in 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/29-10-2025-climate-inaction-is-claiming-millions-of-lives-every-year--warns-new-lancet-countdown-report).

## What each level looks like

All three are our judgment. Losses are counted against yields that [keep rising](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09085-w), so a loss of a fifth need not mean less food than today. The IPCC expects food risks to grow [more severe at 2 °C or more](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGII_SummaryForPolicymakers.pdf), with malnutrition concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, Central and South America and small islands.
- **−10 to 5%, level 1.** Food costs a little more; extra hunger stays local.
- **5–30%, level 2.** Tens to hundreds of millions more go short, mostly through prices.
- **30–50%, level 3.** Food per person likely falls below today's: over a tenth hungry. The edge is 30%, not 20%, because a fifth or a quarter comes off a rising baseline.

## Where the 2100 odds come from

Nobody publishes odds. The study's moderate scenario (RCP4.5) has the same end-of-century forcing as the IPCC middle scenario, which matches current policies at 2.7 °C ([Lenton et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41893-023-01132-6)), so we read 11% as the median. The study compares with a world whose climate is [held fixed](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09085-w), so warming already seen is not counted, and its main results leave out the fertilizing effect of extra CO₂.

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

Faster breeding and better farm advice could cut losses. New varieties still need years of field trials, and the heat limits of plants are biological.
