## What it is

The planetary boundaries framework, first proposed in 2009, names nine processes that regulate the Earth system, such as climate, ocean chemistry and the web of life. For each, scientists pick one or two measurable "control variables" and set a boundary. Inside it lies the "safe operating space", where the risk of destabilizing the planet is low. Beyond it lies a zone of increasing risk, and then a high-risk zone where large and possibly irreversible change becomes likely ([Planetary Health Check 2026](https://publications.pik-potsdam.de/pubman/item/item_35009/component/file_35022/PlanetaryHealthCheck2026.pdf)). The 2026 check assesses 13 control variables across the nine processes.

## Why it matters

The boundaries are drawn around the conditions of the last 11,700 years, the stable Holocene period in which farming and cities developed. Crossing one does not trigger instant disaster. It means leaving the conditions we know work, with rising odds of large shifts. The processes also interact: warming worsens ocean acidification and biodiversity loss, and forest loss feeds back on climate and water.

## Where it stands

The 2026 Planetary Health Check finds **7 of 9** boundaries crossed, and all seven are getting worse:

- **Climate change** (strongly crossed): CO₂ at 426 ppm against a boundary of 350.
- **Biosphere integrity** (strongly crossed): extinction rates above 100 per million species per year, against 10.
- **Land system change:** forest cover below safe levels.
- **Freshwater change:** both river flows and soil moisture disrupted.
- **Biogeochemical flows** (strongly crossed): nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilizer.
- **Novel entities** (strongly crossed): synthetic chemicals and plastics released faster than they can be tested.
- **Ocean acidification:** crossed for the first time in the 2025 assessment.

Two remain inside their limits: **stratospheric ozone**, recovering thanks to international bans on ozone-destroying chemicals though still below mid-20th-century levels, and **aerosol loading**, safe globally and improving, but over the regional limit in South Asia.

No study projects this count to 2100. Our judgment: it most likely stays at seven. CO₂ stays above 350 ppm in every IPCC scenario for 2100 ([Jiang et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1029/2022MS003563)), and ocean chemistry recovers only on the lowest path.

## The lines that matter

The framework's own line is each individual boundary; the site shows the count. Our two lines on that count:

- **Eight of nine:** ozone or aerosols would have to fail again. That would take a new shock, such as large-scale injection of particles into the stratosphere to cool the planet, which could harm ozone. Chance by 2100: about 15% (judgment).
- **Nine of nine:** both at once. About 3% (judgment).

The count can also mislead. A boundary barely crossed counts the same as one deep in the high-risk zone. The ocean acidification line itself is uncertain: one study puts today's value inside the boundary's error range rather than clearly past it ([Findlay et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.70238)).

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

The ozone story shows that boundaries can be restored when the fix is clear and cheap. SI could make substitutes cheaper and monitoring better for other cases too: low-fertilizer farming, safer chemicals, faster decarbonization.

But most boundaries are stocks that change over decades: CO₂ in the air, species lost, forests cleared, chemicals already spread. SI cannot rewind those quickly, and several depend on land and politics more than on technology.
