## What it is

The International Energy Agency (IEA) compares expected copper demand in 2035 with the supply that existing mines and announced projects could deliver. The gap is the share of demand left uncovered. In practice a "deficit" never shows up as empty shelves. Prices rise until some demand is cancelled, delayed, or switched to other materials such as aluminium.

## Why it matters

Copper is in power lines, transformers, motors, wind turbines, solar farms and electric cars. A lasting shortfall would make electrification slower and more expensive. It would not cut off food or water by itself, but it would slow the energy shift that both climate safety and cheaper energy depend on.

## Where it stands

In its July 2026 outlook, the IEA projects that primary copper supply could fall about 25% short of demand in 2035 under today's policy settings ([ANI via Times of Oman](https://timesofoman.com/article/174904-copper-supply-may-face-25-deficit-by-2035-despite-record-prices-iea)). That is narrower than the 30% shortfall in the 2025 edition ([Global Trade Review](https://www.gtreview.com/news/global/copper-market-faces-major-risks-at-supply-shortage-looms/); [Hydrocarbon Processing](https://www.hydrocarbonprocessing.com/news/2025/05/low-diversity-in-critical-mineral-markets-could-hurt-industry-iea-says/)), because new projects in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Zambia advanced ([ANI via The Tribune](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/business/critical-mineral-concerns-shift-from-meeting-demand-to-securing-supply-chains-iea/)). In a net-zero scenario, the 2025 edition put the gap at 40% ([Global Trade Review](https://www.gtreview.com/news/global/copper-market-faces-major-risks-at-supply-shortage-looms/)).

Supply is getting harder to find. Average ore grades have fallen 40% since 1991. The capital cost of expanding existing mines is up 65% since 2020. Only 5% of the deposits found in the past 35 years were found in the last decade ([ANI via Times of Oman](https://timesofoman.com/article/174904-copper-supply-may-face-25-deficit-by-2035-despite-record-prices-iea)). Copper investment did rise 8% in 2025 ([Anadolu](https://aa.com.tr/en/economy/explainer-why-the-iea-says-critical-minerals-have-become-a-65-trillion-economic-security-risk/4000476)). China produced 45% of refined copper in 2024. The IEA expected that to reach 50% by 2040, with demand growing by about a quarter ([Global Trade Review](https://www.gtreview.com/news/global/copper-market-faces-major-risks-at-supply-shortage-looms/)).

Time is the main constraint. Major mines that opened between 2010 and 2019 took more than 16 years on average from discovery to first output ([IEA 2021, via Industrial Info Resources](https://www.industrialinfo.com/news/article/iea-report-identifies-critical-minerals-as-a-weak-spot-in-the-energy-transition--297057)). A deposit found today would typically start producing in the 2040s. No published projection runs to 2100.

## The lines that matter

- **30%, the IEA's 2025 projection.** A gap this large would mean sustained price spikes and delayed grid projects.
- **40%, the net-zero gap.** The IEA's 2025 figure for a net-zero pathway. Above it, copper rather than policy would limit how fast the world can electrify.

These are reference points chosen for this site from IEA projections, not official thresholds.

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

Superintelligence (SI) could find deposits faster, improve processing of low-grade ore, raise recycling rates, and design products that need less copper or use aluminium instead. It cannot shorten the physical work of permitting, financing and building a mine, which has taken more than 16 years on average. Substitution and recycling are where SI could help soonest. New mines are where it helps least.
