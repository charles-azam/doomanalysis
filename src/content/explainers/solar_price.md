## What it is

The spot price of a standard crystalline-silicon solar module, in US dollars per watt of rated power. The site uses InfoLink's average price for TOPCon modules, the most common type sold today. Modules are only part of the cost of a solar farm. Land, labour, inverters, grid connection and finance make up the rest.

## Why it matters

Energy is the input behind water pumping, fertiliser, cooling and industry. If panels keep getting cheaper, all of these get cheaper too, which is central to any claim that essentials could become nearly free. IRENA finds that over 90% of new large renewable projects in 2025 cost less than the cheapest new fossil-fuel option ([PV Tech](https://www.pv-tech.org/solar-pv-lcoe-ticks-marginally-upwards-2025-irena/)). A lasting price reversal would make the energy transition slower and costlier.

## Where it stands

On 18 November 2026, InfoLink's average TOPCon module price was US$0.114 per watt ([InfoLink](https://www.infolink-group.com/spot-price/)). Trade rules matter. TOPCon modules assembled in the US cost $0.31/W delivered, against $0.135/W assembled in India and $0.115/W for Chinese-made modules shipped to Europe (same source). The industry roadmap ITRPV puts the end-2025 module price near $0.09/W, slightly up on end-2024 after steep falls in 2023 and 2024 ([PV Tech](https://www.pv-tech.org/itrpv-2026-solar-industry-maintains-historic-learning-curve-despite-market-turbulence/)).

The long-run trend is a learning curve. From 1976 to 2025, each doubling of all modules ever produced cut the price by about 26% on average ([ITRPV via PV Tech](https://www.pv-tech.org/itrpv-2026-solar-industry-maintains-historic-learning-curve-despite-market-turbulence/)). The IEA estimates that a gigawatt of solar cost about $3 billion to build in 2015 and about $0.7 billion in 2025 ([Down To Earth](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/energy/clean-energy-investment-saved-fuel-importers-260-billion-in-2025-iea)). IRENA puts the world's average cost of electricity from new solar at $44 per megawatt-hour in 2025 ([PV Tech](https://www.pv-tech.org/solar-pv-lcoe-ticks-marginally-upwards-2025-irena/)).

Today's prices are partly a sign of distress. Chinese factories ran at about 40–60% of capacity in July 2026, and polysilicon, the raw material, sold below average production cost ([Caixin](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-09-04/china-moves-to-rein-in-solar-price-war-as-oversupply-batters-industry-102481572.html)). In July 2026 regulators told manufacturers to stop selling below cost, without setting a price floor ([pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/08/04/china-draws-a-line-under-the-solar-price-war/)). A near-term rise would therefore not mean the learning curve has stopped.

Further out, DNV projected in 2023 that the cost of solar electricity would roughly halve to $21/MWh by 2050, with the learning rate slowing from 26% to 17% ([PV Tech](https://www.pv-tech.org/dnv-solar-pvs-lcoe-to-halve-by-2050)). No one publishes a module-price forecast to 2100.

## The lines that matter

No official danger line exists. This site uses two judgment lines:

- **$0.20/W, price reversal.** Close to double today's price, a sign of a supply shock or a split in world trade.
- **$0.50/W, supply shock.** Panels several times dearer, making solar far less competitive.

At the other end, around $0.05/W, the site treats module cost as close to negligible.

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

Materials discovery, cell design and factory optimisation are areas where superintelligence (SI) should help most, so further price falls are plausible. SI cannot remove the other costs: land, grid connections, permits, storage for the night, and the politics of trade. Cheap modules do not make electricity free at the plug.
