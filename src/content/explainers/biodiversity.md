## What it is

The Living Planet Index follows tens of thousands of monitored wildlife populations (mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish) and averages how much each has grown or shrunk since 1970. The 2026 edition covers 35,803 populations of 5,790 species ([Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/data-insights/the-living-planet-index-reports-a-73-average-decline-in-studied-wildlife-populations)).

It is an average of changes, not a headcount. A 73% decline does not mean 73% of animals or species have disappeared. About half of the populations tracked are stable or increasing; others have collapsed. Because it is a weighted geometric mean, it is easy to misread, and its method is debated.

## Why it matters

Wild species pollinate crops, filter water, build soils, store carbon and feed people through fisheries. When populations shrink, ecosystems become less able to absorb shocks such as drought, fire or disease. That matters for food and water security long before any species goes extinct.

The planetary-boundary scientists measure the same pressure differently. Species are going extinct at over 100 per million species per year, against a safe level of 10, and people take 21–30% of the energy plants produce, against a safe level of 10% ([Planetary Health Check 2026](https://publications.pik-potsdam.de/pubman/item/item_35009/component/file_35022/PlanetaryHealthCheck2026.pdf)).

## Where it stands

The 2026 Living Planet Report finds an average **73% decline** in monitored populations from 1970 to 2022, about 2.5% a year. Freshwater populations fell most (85%), then land (69%) and ocean (59%). By region, Latin America and the Caribbean lost 95% and Africa 80%, against 28% in North America and 23% in Europe and Central Asia ([summary of the report](https://www.drishtiias.com/daily-updates/daily-news-analysis/living-planet-report-2026)). The Planetary Health Check names habitat conversion, resource extraction, pollution and climate change among the main pressures.

There is no published forecast of this index for 2100. Climate change adds a growing risk on top of habitat loss. A large review of extinction studies estimates that about 1.6% of species face extinction from climate change at today's warming, about 1 in 20 at 2.7 °C, and 29.7% at 5.4 °C ([Urban 2024](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1066448)).

## The lines that matter

No agency sets a threshold on this index, so both lines are our own.

- **80% decline:** the average monitored population at one-fifth of its 1970 size. Staying below it needs the index to fall less than 0.4% a year to 2100, about a sixth of the past pace. Chance of crossing by 2100: about 70% (judgment).
- **90% decline:** one-tenth of 1970 size. It needs about half the past pace, sustained to 2100. Chance: about 35% (judgment).

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

SI can help where the problem is information and efficiency: tracking populations, spotting poaching and illegal logging, and above all growing more food on less land, which eases the biggest driver, habitat conversion.

It cannot bring back extinct species or old-growth ecosystems on human timescales. Recovery runs on ecological time, not computing time. Whether land is freed for nature is also a political choice, not a technical one.
