## What it is

The total fertility rate is the number of children a woman would have if she lived through her childbearing years at this year's birth rates for each age ([World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SP.DYN.TFRT.IN)). It is a snapshot of one year, not a count of children actually born to any group of women. When people delay births, it can dip, then partly recover.

## Why it matters

At about 2.1 children per woman, each generation replaces itself in the long run, if there is no migration ([UN Summary of Results](https://population.un.org/wpp/assets/Files/WPP2024_Summary-of-Results.pdf)). Well below that, populations shrink and age, which strains pensions and care (see aging). Well above it, populations grow fast, which raises demand for food, water, jobs and schools in the countries least able to supply them.

## Where it stands

The world is at 2.25 children per woman, down from 3.31 in 1990. More than half of all countries are below 2.1. Nearly a fifth, including China, Italy, South Korea and Spain, are at "ultra-low" levels below 1.4. For 24 of these whose population has already peaked, the UN puts the chance of returning to 2.1 within 30 years at 0.1% ([UN Summary of Results](https://population.un.org/wpp/assets/Files/WPP2024_Summary-of-Results.pdf)).

France is at 1.61 and South Korea at 0.75. Nigeria is at 4.38 and DR Congo at 5.98 ([World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SP.DYN.TFRT.IN), 2024).

The UN medium projection has the world at 1.84 in 2100, with an 80% range of 1.69 to 2.05. France is at 1.65, with a range of 1.25 to 2.03 ([WPP 2024](https://population.un.org/wpp/assets/Excel%20Files/1_Indicator%20(Standard)/CSV_FILES/WPP2024_Demographic_Indicators_Medium.csv.gz); [probabilistic](https://population.un.org/wpp/assets/Excel%20Files/2_Indicators%20(Probabilistic)/EXCEL_FILES/3_Fertility/UN_PPP2024_Input_TFR.xlsx)). The projection assumes very low-fertility countries partly recover: South Korea reaches 1.30. World population peaks at about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s, and the UN gives an 80% chance that it peaks this century.

## The lines that matter

Both lines are published by the UN.

- **2.1 (replacement).** Below it, each generation is smaller. From the UN quantiles, the world has about a 93% chance of being below 2.1 in 2100, and France the same.
- **1.4 ("ultra-low").** Below it, each generation is roughly a third smaller and ageing is fast. France has about a 21% chance of being below 1.4 in 2100. South Korea, already far below, has about a 59% chance of still being under it. The world's chance is well under 1%.

Low fertility is a slow pressure, not a sudden collapse. Migration can offset part of it.

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

Fertility mostly reflects what people want and can afford: housing, time, careers, partners. Superintelligence could cut the cost of raising children, in money and in time, and it could improve fertility treatment. Neither guarantees more births, because rich societies with generous support still sit below 2.1. The UN warns that policies to raise fertility will have less and less effect on population size. In countries that peaked, the number of women of childbearing age is set to fall by a third by 2054. Superintelligence cannot undo that momentum within this century.
