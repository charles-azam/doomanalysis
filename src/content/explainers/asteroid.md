## Why it matters here

A small asteroid bursts high in the air. A large one throws up enough dust and smoke to dim the sun for years, so harvests fail everywhere. It is one of the few hazards that can reach level 5 alone: the [10 km asteroid of 66 million years ago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_event) is blamed for a mass extinction. The chance is tiny, and sky surveys have [cut the expected toll](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12842/chapter/4) further.

## In numbers

Each chance is 1 − e^(−75 ÷ interval), for the 75 years to 2100.

- **25 m, [every 200 years](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12842/chapter/4):** 1 − e^(−75/200) = 31%. **50 m, every 2,000 years:** 3.7%.
- **140 m:** population models give [0.00004 impacts a year](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.02418v2), so 0.3% by 2100. NASA has found [11,343 and expects 14,000 more](https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/planetary-science/planetary-defense/near-earth-asteroids/), and [no known asteroid threatens Earth for 100 years](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/is-nasa-aware-of-any-earth-threatening-asteroids-we-asked-a-nasa-scientist-episode-14). Only unfound ones count: 0.3% × 14,000 ÷ 25,343 = 0.17%.
- **1 km, every 700,000 years:** 75 ÷ 700,000 = 0.011%. NASA has found 873 and expects 50 more: 0.011% × 50 ÷ 923 = 0.0006%. Toby Ord's [1 in 120,000 per century](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/D8816A79BEF5A4C30A3E44FD8D768622) agrees.
- **10 km, every 100 million years:** 0.00008%, before counting that the big ones are tracked.

## What each level looks like

- **10–25 m, level 0.** Most [burn up, doing little or no damage](https://www.nasa.gov/solar-system/asteroids/asteroid-fast-facts/). Chelyabinsk, about 20 m, [injured some 1,500 people and killed none](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_event).
- **25 m–1 km, level 1.** NASA expects [local damage](https://www.nasa.gov/solar-system/asteroids/asteroid-fast-facts/); the NRC grades it from local to continental ([table 2.1](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12842/chapter/4)). Tunguska, 50–80 m, [flattened 500,000 acres of forest](https://www.nasa.gov/solar-system/tunguska-revisited-111-year-old-mystery-impact-inspires-new-more-optimistic-asteroid-predictions/).
- **1–1.5 km, level 2.** Our judgment: the NRC calls 1 km a possible global catastrophe, and its model starts at a tenth of humanity at 1.5 km.
- **1.5–5 km, level 3.** Derived: in that model, deaths [rise from 10% at 1.5 km to everyone at 10 km](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12842/chapter/4). Rising evenly on a log scale, 5 km gives 10^(−1 + log(5/1.5) ÷ log(10/1.5)) = 43%.
- **5–10 km, level 4.** Our judgment: that line passes 50% near 5.6 km.
- **Over 10 km, level 5.** The model [leaves no survivors](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12842/chapter/4).

## Where the 2100 odds come from

The four points are the calculations above. For 140 m we use the rate shared by [Harris and Chodas and by Nesvorný's team](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.02418v2); Nugent's team finds twice that, which gives 0.38%. [Another table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_event) puts 50 m impacts every 764 years. We publish no point at 5 or 10 km: known kilometre-sized objects [should miss for 1,000 years](https://www.space.com/earth-probably-safe-devastating-asteroid-impact-thousand-years), and the remaining threat is mostly [long-period comets, with little warning](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12842/chapter/4).

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

It could find the remaining objects sooner and plan deflection missions. A deflection still needs years of warning and a rocket built in time, and a comet may not allow that.
