## What it is

The share of a country's energy use that it buys abroad, net of what it sells. The [World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/EG.IMP.CONS.ZS) computes it as energy use minus domestic production, divided by energy use. A negative number means a net exporter. Norway's −729% means it exports more than seven times the energy it uses at home.

## Why it matters

Energy is one of the five essentials, and it also runs the other four: pumps for water, tractors and fertilizer for food, hospitals, and the police. An importer depends on its suppliers, on shipping routes and on world prices. A long cut-off or a price shock can leave homes cold and trucks idle. Exporters face the mirror risk: their budgets depend on fuel sales.

## Where it stands

In 2024 ([World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/EG.IMP.CONS.ZS)):

- Japan 87%, Germany 70%, United Kingdom 48%, France 42%, India 36%, Sweden 28%, China 24%
- Net exporters: United States −10%, Brazil −11%, Nigeria −83%, Norway −729%

**France** is less exposed than it was, but still exposed:

- Its import share fell from 54% in 2022 to 42% in 2024.
- France produced 61.0% of the energy it consumed in 2024 ([SDES](https://www.statistiques.developpement-durable.gouv.fr/bilan-energetique-de-la-france-pour-2024)) and 62.7% in 2025, its best level since 1970 ([SDES](https://www.statistiques.developpement-durable.gouv.fr/bilan-energetique-de-la-france-en-2025-donnees-provisoires)).
- Its energy import bill was €57.7 billion in 2024: €43.7 billion for oil and €17.4 billion for gas. It fell to €45.8 billion in 2025.
- Oil (39%) and gas (18%) still make up most of the energy French homes, vehicles and firms use.

**Nuclear power hides a dependence.** France needs 6,000 to 8,000 tonnes of natural uranium a year and imports all of it. In 2023, 98% by value came from Kazakhstan, Namibia, Niger and Australia ([Connaissance des Énergies](https://www.connaissancedesenergies.org/questions-et-reponses-energies/dou-vient-luranium-naturel-importe-en-france)). Energy statistics count the heat from reactors as domestic production, so this uranium does not appear in the 42%. Uranium is compact and can be stockpiled, so a supply cut hurts far more slowly than one in oil or gas.

**Electrification changes the picture.** France's 2026 energy plan aims to replace costly imported fossil fuels with low-carbon electricity. It targets 380–420 TWh of nuclear output in 2030, up from 320 TWh in 2023 ([AFP](https://www.connaissancedesenergies.org/afp/ppe-3-le-decret-sur-la-feuille-de-route-energetique-publie-au-jo-260213)). Each electric car or heat pump swaps imported oil or gas for power made at home.

Nobody publishes import projections to 2100.

## The lines that matter

- **0%, net importer.** EU law obliges members to hold oil stocks of 90 days of net imports or 61 days of consumption, whichever is larger ([Directive 2009/119/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32009L0119)). That cushion buys time, not independence.
- **50%, limit.** Our judgment: above it, most of the energy supply depends on foreign decisions.
- **75%, danger.** Our judgment: a major supplier crisis could not be covered from domestic sources. Japan is above this line.

## What superintelligence can and cannot change

Superintelligence (SI) could speed the shift to electricity: cheaper batteries, better grids, faster design of solar, wind, geothermal or fusion plants. Most countries could then make their energy at home.

It cannot change geology or how much sun and wind a country gets, and power plants and grids still take years to build. Dependence may also move rather than vanish, from fuels to imported panels, batteries and the minerals inside them.
